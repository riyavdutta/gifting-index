Method

What this can and can’t tell you

The data

Google searches are the main signal: weekly and monthly Google Trends interest, worldwide, for “Jo Malone”, “Diptyque”, “Le Labo” and “Byredo”, collected through SerpApi. Google scales each search series 0–100 against the busiest point across all four, so the site compares each brand with its own usual level.

Wikipedia pageviews are the second signal: daily human views (bots excluded) of each brand’s English article. Daily data is what the live season and Moments need.

Both are fetched once every morning and saved with the site, so a slow or failed fetch never breaks the page: it simply shows the last good copy and its date.

Le Labo’s and Byredo’s articles start in 2017, so their Wikipedia seasons count from 2018. Diptyque’s article was missing or renamed from March to August 2020; that stretch is left out rather than counted as zero.

What it measures

Interest, not sales. Searches lean towards intent: people looking up where to buy, or what to give. Wikipedia views lean towards curiosity: news, controversy, or who a brand is. Where the two signals agree, the finding is stronger.

Google Trends is worldwide; Wikipedia is English-language only, so it under-counts interest in Diptyque’s home market, France, and Byredo’s, Sweden.

The live season compares each year with its own late summer (15 Aug to 30 Sep), so a brand that’s growing overall isn’t mistaken for a strong Christmas.

What didn’t hold up

An early version used monthly averages and found a March bump for Jo Malone, which looked like Mother’s Day in the UK. Switching to medians made it disappear: a few one-off spikes had been pulling the average up. The December pattern survived both methods, so it’s the one reported here.
